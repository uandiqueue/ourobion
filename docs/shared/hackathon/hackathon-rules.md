About the challenge
The Launchpad AI Challenge is a four-week, open-format AI build sprint. It is also a deliberate redesign of the career fair.
At a career fair, you get minutes with a recruiter and a resume that looks like everyone else's. Hackathons are no better: a handful of judges triaging 50 to 100+ submissions in an hour, where well-reasoned design decisions lose to flashy demos that might not even work.
We fixed both. Your judges are your potential employers. Over 100 professionals will judge this challenge: founders, engineers, researchers, product leaders, and domain specialists in fields like robotics, agentic systems, and applied ML. Every submission is algorithmically matched to the judges best suited to evaluate it. Your robotics project lands in front of robotics people. Your research lands in front of researchers.
Every judge has the time to read your reasoning and check your evidence. When they review your project, they unlock your full profile: your work, your reasoning, and who you are beyond the resume. The question each judge answers is simple:
"Is this person sharp and thoughtful enough that I'd want to interview them?"
That question gets answered for every matched submission, not just the finalists.
What to build
Anything serious, built with AI:
Applications: products and tools solving real problems for real users
Agentic systems: multi-step, tool-using, autonomous or semi-autonomous workflows
Embodied AI & robotics: perception, control, sim-to-real, hardware-in-the-loop
Deep learning research: new methods, architectures, training strategies, or rigorous empirical studies
Infrastructure & tooling: evals, observability, data pipelines, developer tools for AI
You will not be judged against projects from other categories. You will be judged by domain-relevant experts on the quality of your thinking within your own domain.
How you will be judged
Five pillars, each scored 1 to 5:
Problem. Why does this matter, and what would success look like? Precise problem statement, why existing approaches fall short, success criteria defined before you built.
Approach. Why this solution, and what did you rule out? Every major decision reasoned, alternatives named. Simplicity with justification beats complexity without it.
Evidence. How do you know it works? Every claim backed by a measurement, comparison, or demonstration. Compared against a baseline, not reported in isolation.
Constraints. Does it work under real-world limits? Cost, latency, compute, reliability, safety. Measure trade-offs rather than assuming more resources equals better.
Honesty & Trajectory. Where does it break, and what is next? Known failure modes, honest limitations, a concrete next-steps plan.
The one rule: evidence appropriate to the claim. You choose what to claim, and your evidence must match the size of your claim. A modest claim, proven, beats a grand claim, asserted. Blanket statements are the fastest way to lose a judge.
After scoring, every judge answers one final question: would I want to interview this person?
What to submit
Repository. Your code, public or with judge access granted.
Demo video. Maximum 3 minutes. Mandatory for all tracks.
Write-up. Maximum 1,000 words, using the five pillars as section headings. Appendices do not count against the cap.
Each participant also completes a profile at registration: an introduction, a short message to judges, and your resume. Judges see it when they unlock your submission. It is your interview before the interview.

Launchpad 2026 AI Challenge — Judging Criteria
This page defines how every submission is evaluated.

1. How judging works
Every submission is algorithmically matched to judges whose expertise fits the work. Robotics projects go to robotics people. Research goes to researchers. Products go to operators who ship.

Each judge:

Reviews your submission in this order: write-up, demo video, repository spot-checks.
Scores five pillars, each from 1 to 5.
Answers one final, holistic question: "Would I want to interview this person?" (Yes / Lean yes / No)
The interview verdict is independent of the score. A mid-ranked project with rigorous, honest thinking can earn a Yes. A polished demo with no reasoning behind it can earn a No. This verdict is answered for every matched submission, not just the finalists.

2. The one rule: evidence appropriate to the claim
You choose what to claim. Your evidence must match the size of your claim.

Claim "production-ready" and you will be held to production standards.
Claim "a proof of concept with one novel insight" and you will be held to the rigour of that insight.
A modest claim, proven, beats a grand claim, asserted. Every single time.
You are judged within the standards of your own domain, never against another category's artefact. A research submission is never penalised for lacking a UI. An application is never penalised for lacking novel theory. Judges score the quality of your reasoning and evidence, nothing else.

Blanket statements are the fastest way to lose a judge. "We used the most capable model, so it's accurate" is not evidence. "We chose a smaller model, and here is the cost-accuracy curve that justified it" is the kind of sentence that gets you an interview.

3. The five pillars
Pillar 1: Problem
"A problem well stated is a problem half solved." — Charles Kettering

Why does this matter, and what would success look like?

We expect a precise problem statement directed at the heart of what your project is. Why are existing approaches insufficient? What are your success criteria, defined before you built? Have you accounted for what is specific and unique to your domain?

Score	Description
1	No clear problem. The project exists because the technology exists.
2	A problem is named, but it is generic. No examination of existing approaches.
3	Problem stated and plausible. Success criteria exist but are vague or defined after the fact. Existing approaches mentioned but not seriously examined.
4	Precise problem statement with clear success criteria. Existing approaches examined and their gaps identified.
5	All of the above, plus a non-obvious insight about the domain that visibly shaped the design. The team understands this problem better than the average person would.
By track. Applications: a real user pain, and why current tools fail them. Agentic systems: why this task needs an agent at all, rather than a single well-prompted call. Embodied AI: the physical constraint or capability gap being addressed. Research: the gap in prior work, positioned against actual literature. Infra and tooling: the workflow bottleneck, and who suffers from it today.

Pillar 2: Approach
"The essence of strategy is choosing what not to do." — Michael Porter

Why this solution, and what did you rule out?

Reason with every major decision. What are the alternatives, and where are their shortcomings? Simplicity with justification beats complexity without it.

Score	Description
1	Design decisions unexplained. Complexity without purpose, or the obvious default with no thought.
2	The approach is described but not justified. No alternatives mentioned.
3	Main decisions justified, but few or no alternatives considered.
4	Every major decision reasoned. At least one credible alternative named per decision, with its shortcomings.
5	All of the above, plus deliberate simplicity where it wins. The design shows taste: what was left out is as considered as what was built. A judge could defend these choices to a colleague.
By track. Applications: architecture and scope decisions, build-vs-use choices. Agentic systems: why this orchestration, this model size, this tool set; what a simpler pipeline would miss. Embodied AI: sensor, control, and sim-vs-real choices. Research: method design, baselines chosen, ablation plan. Infra and tooling: interface and abstraction decisions, what was deliberately not supported.

Pillar 3: Evidence
"Talk is cheap. Show me the code." — Linus Torvalds

How do you know it works?

Back your claims with a measurement, comparison, or demonstration appropriate to the claim. Does each claim have evidence attached, or is it asserted? Is the evidence matched to a baseline or alternative, not just reported in isolation?

Score	Description
1	Claims asserted, not demonstrated. Demo-only proof.
2	Some measurements exist, but they are disconnected from the claims being made.
3	Claims measured, but with no baseline or comparison. Small or cherry-picked samples.
4	Every claim traces to evidence. Compared against a sensible baseline, including the simple one.
5	All of the above, plus statistical honesty: sample sizes, variance, and edge cases addressed. The evaluation itself is well designed and could be reproduced.
By track. Applications: task success rates, user testing, error rates on realistic inputs. Agentic systems: end-to-end task completion on a held-out set, trajectory analysis, comparison against the single-call baseline. Embodied AI: trials in sim and reality, success rates under perturbation. Research: benchmarks against published baselines, ablations, reproducible scripts. Infra and tooling: before-and-after measurements on the workflow it claims to improve.

Pillar 4: Constraints
"There are no solutions, there are only trade-offs." — Thomas Sowell

Does it work under real-world limits?

Cost, latency, compute, reliability, safety: whichever are native to your problem. Show you measured trade-offs rather than assuming more resources equals better.

Score	Description
1	No discussion of any real-world limit. Assumes more resources means better.
2	Constraints named but neither measured nor designed for.
3	Constraints acknowledged, with rough or partial measurement.
4	Key trade-offs measured: cost against performance, latency against quality, or the equivalents native to the domain.
5	All of the above, plus resource choices justified with data. The team can show the curve, not just the point they chose on it.
By track. Applications: unit economics, latency budgets, reliability under load. Agentic systems: price-to-performance and latency-to-performance quantified; which frontier model you used matters far less than whether you can justify it. Embodied AI: real-time constraints, hardware limits, safety margins. Research: compute budget, sample efficiency, scaling behaviour. Infra and tooling: overhead introduced, failure behaviour, operational cost.

Pillar 5: Honesty & Trajectory
"The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool." — Richard Feynman

Where does it break, and what is next?

What are the known failure modes, and where does the approach degrade? What is the concrete next-steps plan?

Score	Description
1	No limitations discussed. Failure modes hidden or unknown.
2	A token limitations line with no specifics.
3	Generic limitations section. Vague next steps.
4	Specific known failure modes and where the approach degrades, with examples.
5	All of the above, plus reported negative results, and a concrete, credible plan for what the team would do with two more weeks. The team knows their own work's edges better than the judge could find them.
By track. This pillar reads the same everywhere. Honest self-assessment looks identical whether the artefact is a robot, a paper, or a product. It is also the pillar judges consistently say impresses them most.

4. Green flags and red flags
Judges are briefed on these explicitly.

What impresses:

Reported negative results and honest limitations
Beating the simple baseline before building the complex thing
Ablations, trade-off curves, cost and latency measurements
A cheaper or simpler approach chosen with data to back it
Knowing exactly what you would do with two more weeks
What loses judges:

"We used the most capable model, so it's accurate"
Five cherry-picked demo runs presented as an eval
No baseline, no alternatives, no cost discussion
Complexity for its own sake
"It works" without defining what working means
Judges are instructed not to be seduced by demo polish. A well-reasoned design decision beats a flashy demo that might not work. This is the exact failure of normal hackathon judging that this challenge exists to fix.

5. Fairness across tracks
Scores are comparable across the wide spectrum of submissions because:

The five pillars are questions, not checklists. Every domain answers them with its own native evidence.
Judges are matched by domain. Your work is read by people qualified to recognise quality in it.
The anchors above define what each score means, so a 3 means the same thing to every judge.
Judges score each pillar independently. A brilliant Approach does not excuse missing Evidence.
6. Practical advice for scoring well
Write your success criteria in week one. Pillar 1 rewards criteria defined before you built. It shows when they are retrofitted.
Keep a decision log. Every time you choose between two approaches, note why. Your Approach section writes itself.
Run the baseline. Compare against the simplest alternative. Beating it is your strongest evidence. Losing to it, and knowing why, is worth more than hiding it.
Measure cost and latency as you go. Logging from the start is free. Retrofitting in the last week is painful.
Save your failures. Dead ends become your Honesty & Trajectory section.
Respect the word cap. Your write-up is 1,000 words. Every sentence should either state a claim, justify a decision, or point to evidence. Put charts and logs in appendices.
The rubric is not a hurdle. It is a description of how strong engineers and researchers already think. Show us that, and the scores take care of themselves.
